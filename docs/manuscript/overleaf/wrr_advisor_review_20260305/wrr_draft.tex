\title{Gauge-Informed Residual Correction of National Water Model Streamflow in Southern Appalachian Catchments}

\authors{M. Carson\affil{1}, M. A. Javidian\affil{1}, W. P. Anderson Jr.\affil{2}}

\begin{keypoints}
\item Gauge-informed correction lowers streamflow error at three unregulated southern Appalachian basins
\item Recent gauge history provides most of the improvement, while model output adds routing context
\item Skill degrades at the regulated river site, indicating the need for dam-operation inputs
\end{keypoints}

\begin{abstract}
Physics-anchored residual correction offers a practical way to improve local guidance from the National Water Model (NWM) while retaining its hydrologic backbone. We evaluate a gauge-informed residual correction framework at three unregulated stream gauges in the southern Appalachian region using chronological train, validation, and test splits (2010--2017, 2018, and 2019--2020). The model combines lagged United States Geological Survey (USGS) discharge, NWM discharge, and ERA5-Land meteorological forcings to predict site-specific NWM residuals. On the 2019--2020 test period, root mean square error (RMSE) decreases by 45.3\% to 56.0\%, and Nash--Sutcliffe efficiency increases from 0.431--0.617 for raw NWM to 0.804--0.926 for the corrected product. Input ablation shows that lagged gauge observations provide most of the improvement, while NWM inputs add 12.0--44.3 percentage points of additional RMSE reduction beyond a gauge-plus-meteorology configuration. A gauge-free surrogate using NWM plus meteorology alone still reduces RMSE by 5.0\%--21.8\%. Persistence remains stronger for one-hour point prediction, so the contribution of the proposed framework is not to replace autoregressive nowcasts but to provide a physically anchored correction layer with event-focused error reduction and a clear boundary condition for regulated rivers. At a fourth, regulated site, correction skill degrades without dam-operation covariates.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Plain Language Summary}
The National Water Model is NOAA's system for estimating river flow across the United States, but errors at individual gauges can still be large during storms. We tested whether recent river-gauge measurements can be used to correct those local errors at three unregulated rivers in the southern Appalachians.

A model that combined recent gauge history with National Water Model output and weather inputs reduced streamflow error by 45\% to 56\% during 2019--2020. Improvements were largest during high-flow periods, when better local guidance matters most for flood awareness. Tests that removed one input source at a time showed that recent gauge observations carried most of the predictive value, while the National Water Model still added useful information about routing and basin state.

At a fourth site downstream from a dam, the same approach did not help. That result shows that regulated rivers need information about reservoir releases and dam operations before machine-learning corrections can be trusted. Overall, this study shows that gauge-informed residual correction can improve local guidance from a national hydrologic model in natural mountain basins, while also clarifying where the approach is likely to fail.


%----------------------INTRODUCTION-------------------------------
\section{Introduction}

Physics-based hydrologic models such as the NOAA National Water Model (NWM) provide continental-scale streamflow guidance, but they often suffer from biases and errors unique to each site and hydrologic regime. These systematic residual errors---the difference between model-simulated and observed flows---undermine forecast accuracy at the local scale, motivating the use of data-driven post-processors to correct model output. Prior studies have shown the promise of machine learning in this context: for example, \citeA{frame2021} trained LSTM networks to post-process NWM outputs across 531 basins, achieving broad improvements (median NSE $\approx 0.73$ versus $0.62$ for the raw NWM). Similarly, \citeA{han2022} implemented an LSTM to correct hourly NWM discharge predictions in California's Russian River basin, significantly improving short-lead runoff forecasts. A recent model-agnostic post-processing framework by \citeA{neisary2025}, which incorporated upstream reservoir storage and snowpack data, yielded up to a 65\% increase in KGE and a 33.5\% bias reduction in heavily regulated basins.

In parallel, attention-based sequence models can ingest long histories and identify temporal patterns that may correlate with model error. A Temporal Fusion Transformer was recently shown to outperform LSTM models for rainfall-runoff predictions across thousands of basins \cite{rasiya2023}, and \citeA{demiray2024} demonstrated improved short-term streamflow forecasting with transformer-based architectures. These studies motivate testing whether recurrent-attention hybrids can represent hydrologic error structure more flexibly than standalone recurrent networks.

This need is especially acute in steep southern Appalachian basins, where short response times, complex terrain, and mixed runoff-generation mechanisms can amplify local forecast error during high-flow events. The practical question is not whether machine learning can replace a continental hydrologic model, but whether it can recover site-level skill in a way that remains anchored to the physical forecast trajectory.

This study addresses a central research question: \textit{Can lagged gauge observations improve site-level correction of NWM streamflow error, and how much information is added by the baseline hydrologic model itself?} We hypothesize that lagged USGS observations---representing the observed flow state---provide the dominant signal for next-hour residual prediction, while NWM forecasts contribute complementary process information (routing dynamics and spatial integration) that ERA5 meteorology alone cannot supply.

In this study, we evaluate a hybrid GRU--transformer residual corrector (``Hydra v3'') across four gauges in two southern Appalachian watersheds---the South Fork New River / New River system and the Watauga River basin. Primary claims are restricted to three unregulated sites; a fourth, regulated site downstream of Watauga Dam (Tennessee Valley Authority) is retained as a boundary-condition case. The approach is residual-centric: instead of forecasting streamflow outright, the model learns the correction term $\hat{r}(t) = y_{\mathrm{obs}}(t) - y_{\mathrm{NWM}}(t)$, which is then added back to the NWM forecast. This ensures the corrected hydrograph remains anchored to the physical model's trajectory.

The experimental design emphasizes leakage avoidance: we partition the data chronologically (training on 2010--2017, validating on 2018, testing on 2019--2020) to mimic deployment where future data are unseen. The primary USGS+NWM+ERA5 configuration is evaluated alongside systematic input ablation experiments that remove individual data sources to quantify their contributions. The intent is a regional proof-of-concept and diagnostic analysis of what drives skill, not a claim of broad geographic generalization from a three-site sample.

\subsection{Data Sources and Preprocessing}

We construct a unified, hourly dataset for each site by collocating three data streams:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{USGS Observed Streamflow.} Hourly instantaneous discharge from NWIS, converted to m$^{3}$/s and resampled to a uniform hourly cadence. USGS observations serve two roles: (1) defining the correction target $r_t = Q_{\text{obs},t} - Q_{\text{NWM},t}$, and (2) providing a lagged discharge sequence for hours $t{-}168$ through $t{-}1$ as a dynamic input feature. This 168-h observed flow trajectory allows the model to learn from recent hydrograph dynamics.
    \item \textbf{NWM v2.1 Modeled Streamflow.} Hourly CHRTOUT retrospective analysis from NOAA's NWM archives (2010--2020). We mapped each USGS gauge to its corresponding NWM reach using the USGS-NWM crosswalk table, selecting the COMID closest to the gauge drainage area.
    \item \textbf{ERA5 Meteorological Reanalysis.} Hourly surface variables from ECMWF ERA5-Land \cite{hersbach2020}, sampled from the grid cell nearest to each gauge location. Variables include precipitation, 2-m temperature and dewpoint, surface pressure, 10-m wind components, downward solar radiation, and volumetric soil moisture. Derived features include vapor pressure deficit, relative humidity, wind speed, and cyclical time encodings (sine/cosine of hour-of-day and day-of-year).
\end{enumerate}

All sources are time-aligned via inner join on hourly timestamps (hours with missing NWM or USGS data are dropped). An inverse hyperbolic sine transform $\tilde{Q} = \operatorname{asinh}(Q)$ normalizes flow distributions. Statistical scalers are fit on training data only to prevent leakage. The study period is 2010--2020 with chronological splits: training (2010--2017, 8 years), validation (2018, 1 year), and testing (2019--2020, 2 years).

The residual target is defined as $r_t = Q_{\text{obs},t} - Q_{\text{NWM},t}$, and the corrected streamflow as $\hat{Q}_{\text{corr},t} = Q_{\text{NWM},t} + \hat{r}_t$.

\subsubsection*{Operational Considerations}
This study uses retrospective products (ERA5-Land reanalysis and NWM v2.1 hindcast) as proxies for operational meteorological and model inputs. In deployment, ERA5 would be replaced by numerical weather prediction forcing or NWM operational analysis products, and NWM retrospective by real-time NWM short-range forecasts. We assume inputs are available with approximately 1-h latency, consistent with typical USGS telemetry transmission schedules. The retrospective design allows controlled evaluation of model performance but may overestimate operational skill if real-time forcing products have larger errors than reanalysis.

\subsection{Model Architecture: Hydra v3}

Hydra v3 is a hybrid GRU--transformer \cite{cho2014,vaswani2017} that predicts NWM residuals from a 168-hour (7-day) input window of dynamic features. It introduces three architectural improvements over the v2 baseline:

\subsubsection*{Feature Importance Gate}
Before the GRU encoder, a learned sigmoid gate $\mathbf{g} = \sigma(\text{MLP}(\mathbf{x}))$ reweights input features on a per-timestep basis. This allows the model to upweight precipitation and soil moisture during storm events while dampening irrelevant features during baseflow:
\begin{linenomath*}
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_t = \mathbf{x}_t \odot \sigma\!\left(\mathbf{W}_2 \, \text{GELU}(\mathbf{W}_1 \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_1) + \mathbf{b}_2\right)
\end{equation}
\end{linenomath*}
Dropout ($p=0.1$) is applied within the MLP between the GELU activation and the final linear layer.

\subsubsection*{Gated Multi-Scale Temporal Convolutions}
After the transformer encoder, three parallel 1D convolution branches extract temporal patterns at distinct hydrological timescales:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Short-range} ($k{=}3$, $d{=}2$): rapid storm response (1--6 h)
    \item \textbf{Mid-range} ($k{=}5$, $d{=}6$): rising and falling limbs (6--48 h)
    \item \textbf{Long-range} ($k{=}7$, $d{=}16$): antecedent moisture and recession (2--7 d)
\end{itemize}
Each branch produces a sequence output; the final timestep representation is extracted for gated fusion. A learned gate produces per-sample softmax weights $\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ to fuse the three branches, allowing the model to emphasize the most relevant timescale for each input:
\begin{linenomath*}
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{h}_{\text{ms}} = \sum_{i=1}^{3} w_i \cdot \mathbf{h}_i, \quad \mathbf{w} = \text{softmax}(\text{MLP}(\mathbf{h}_T^{\text{enc}}))
\end{equation}
\end{linenomath*}

\subsubsection*{Regime-Conditioned Bias}
Instead of a single scalar bias, a small MLP conditioned on the fused representation produces a flow-regime-dependent correction:
\begin{linenomath*}
\begin{equation}
    b_{\text{regime}} = \text{MLP}_{\text{bias}}(\mathbf{h}_{\text{fused}})
\end{equation}
\end{linenomath*}
This enables the model to apply different bias corrections during peaks (where NWM typically underpredicts) versus recessions (where NWM may overshoot).

The full pipeline proceeds as: feature gating $\to$ single-layer GRU encoder $\to$ sinusoidal positional encoding $\to$ transformer encoder $\to$ multi-head summary pooling (last, mean, max, attention-pooled, multi-scale fused) $\to$ fusion MLP $\to$ prediction heads. The model outputs a residual mean, heteroscedastic log-variance for Gaussian NLL, and optional quantile estimates ($\tau \in \{0.1, 0.5, 0.9\}$). In the hydrograph analysis, uncertainty is summarized with residual-based 10th--90th percentile bands (Figure~\ref{fig:hydrograph_panel}). For Figure~\ref{fig:hydrograph_panel}, these bands are mapped to discharge as $\hat{Q}_{\tau}(t)=\hat{Q}_{\mathrm{corr}}(t)+\epsilon_{\tau}$, where $\epsilon_{\tau}$ is the $\tau$th percentile of the residual distribution. Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} illustrates the complete architecture.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/wrr_architecture.pdf}
    \caption{Hydra v3 architecture for NWM residual prediction. The model ingests a 168-hour window of lagged USGS discharge, NWM v2.1 streamflow, and ERA5-Land features through a feature importance gate, encodes them with a GRU and 4-layer transformer, then extracts temporal patterns via parallel branches: summary pooling (last, mean, max, attention) and gated multi-scale convolutions (short/mid/long timescales). \subsubsection*{Loss Function}
The composite training loss is:
\begin{linenomath*}
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{NLL}} + \lambda_{\text{NSE}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{NSE}} + \lambda_{\text{KGE}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{KGE}} + \lambda_q \mathcal{L}_q + \lambda_{\text{nonneg}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{nonneg}}
\end{equation}
\end{linenomath*}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{NLL}} = \frac{1}{2}\left[e^{-s}(y - \mu)^2 + s\right]$ is heteroscedastic Gaussian negative log-likelihood with $s = \log\sigma^2$ the predicted log-variance; $\mathcal{L}_{\text{NSE}} = \sum(y - \hat{y})^2 / \sum(y - \bar{y})^2 = 1 - \text{NSE}$ penalizes relative errors; $\mathcal{L}_{\text{KGE}} = (r-1)^2 + (\alpha-1)^2 + (\beta-1)^2$ where $r$ is Pearson correlation, $\alpha = \sigma_{\hat{y}}/\sigma_y$ is the variability ratio, and $\beta = \mu_{\hat{y}}/\mu_y$ is the bias ratio; $\mathcal{L}_q = \sum_{\tau} \rho_\tau(y - \hat{q}_\tau)$ is pinball loss with $\rho_\tau(u) = \max(\tau u, (\tau-1)u)$ for quantiles $\tau \in \{0.1, 0.5, 0.9\}$; and $\mathcal{L}_{\text{nonneg}} = \text{mean}(\text{ReLU}(-\hat{Q})^2)$ penalizes negative predictions. Default weights are $\lambda_{\text{NSE}} = 0.2$, $\lambda_{\text{KGE}} = 0.05$, $\lambda_q = 0.1$, $\lambda_{\text{nonneg}} = 0$ (ablated in architecture experiments).

\subsubsection*{Gauge-Informed Correction and Input Ablation}
The primary contribution of this study is gauge-informed correction of NWM error using lagged USGS observations alongside NWM forecasts and ERA5 reanalysis. The USGS+NWM+ERA5 configuration represents the full telemetry-informed scenario, where the full 168-h USGS discharge sequence (ending at $t{-}1$) is available as a dynamic input, providing recent hydrograph trajectory information while avoiding contemporaneous leakage. This scenario is realistic for near-real-time flood monitoring where telemetry is available.

\textit{Input ablation} experiments systematically remove data sources to quantify their individual contributions. The USGS+ERA5 configuration removes NWM forecasts to isolate the gauge-only signal. The gauge-free surrogate input setting (Table~\ref{tab:input_configs}), evaluated under the architecture variants in Table~\ref{tab:configs}, removes USGS observations entirely and provides a lower bound on correction accuracy when real-time gauge data are unavailable. Additionally, the two architecture configurations in Table~\ref{tab:configs} ablate the non-negativity constraint to assess its impact on performance.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

Model performance is assessed with five standard hydrological metrics computed at hourly resolution on original-scale discharge (m$^{3}$/s): root mean square error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), Nash--Sutcliffe efficiency (NSE), Kling--Gupta efficiency (KGE), and percent bias (PBIAS). Relative improvement is computed as $\Delta\text{RMSE}~[\%] = 100 \times (\text{RMSE}_{\text{NWM}} - \text{RMSE}_{\text{model}}) / \text{RMSE}_{\text{NWM}}$. We also report RMSE skill score relative to NWM, $SS_{RMSE} = 1 - \text{RMSE}_{\text{model}}/\text{RMSE}_{\text{NWM}}$, 95\% moving-block bootstrap confidence intervals for $\Delta$NSE, and Diebold--Mariano tests of predictive accuracy.

Notably, all gauge-free surrogate results are substantially exceeded by the gauge-informed USGS+NWM+ERA5 configuration (Table~\ref{tab:primary_results}), confirming that lagged gauge observations are the most powerful predictor of NWM residuals.

\subsection{Statistical Significance and Confidence Intervals}

Rigorous evaluation confirms that the deterministic gains in the primary configuration are statistically robust (Table~\ref{tab:significance}). Across the three unregulated sites, $SS_{RMSE}$ ranges from 0.4535 to 0.5605 in the gauge-informed configuration, and all site-level Diebold--Mariano tests indicate improvement relative to raw NWM at $p<0.01$. The gauge-free surrogate (NWM+ERA5-only) also shows positive, but smaller, skill and confidence bounds, reinforcing that most of the recoverable one-hour skill comes from lagged gauge information.

\subsection{Regulated Site}

A fourth gauge at Elizabethton, TN (03486000), downstream of Watauga Dam (TVA), was initially included in the study but excluded from the primary results. We tested both Hydra v2 and v3 configurations at this regulated site. The best v2 configuration achieved only a marginal 0.5\% RMSE improvement, while v3 configurations performed similarly or slightly worse. We report the v2 results here as they achieved marginally better (though still negligible) performance, suggesting that regulation effects persistently challenge both architectures. The Hydra v2 physics-constrained configuration (non-negativity penalty) produced a severe 98.1\% RMSE degradation (NSE: 0.45 $\to$ $-$1.14). Dam operations introduce non-stationarity in the flow regime that the current feature set---lacking explicit dam release schedules or reservoir storage levels---cannot represent. This negative result is informative: it demonstrates that ML-based residual correction without process-specific features can be counterproductive at regulated sites.


%----------------------------------DISCUSSION---------------------------------------------
\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Scale and Baseline Effects}

The primary USGS+NWM+ERA5 configuration achieves 45.3\%, 48.2\%, and 56.0\% RMSE reduction at Jefferson, Galax, and Sugar Grove respectively---substantially larger improvements than the gauge-free surrogate configurations (11\%, 22\%, 5\%). The continued dependence of improvement magnitude on NWM baseline accuracy is evident even in the primary results: Jefferson (NWM NSE = 0.43) and Galax (NWM NSE = 0.27) achieve the largest absolute reductions in RMSE, while Sugar Grove (NWM NSE = 0.62) achieves the largest relative reduction. The lagged USGS observation provides the strongest signal at sites with large, systematic NWM errors, because the gap between the observed and modeled flow state is itself a rich predictor of the next-hour residual.

The scale difference between the two New River sites is also instructive. Galax, as a downstream mainstem integrator with a larger drainage area, exhibits the largest absolute NWM errors (RMSE = 77.6~m$^3$/s) but also the largest absolute improvements. The model's ability to achieve 48.2\% RMSE reduction at this scale suggests that Hydra can learn meaningful error patterns at both headwater and mainstem sites, though the physical interpretation of residuals differs: at headwater sites, errors are dominated by precipitation and runoff generation, while at mainstem sites, routing and aggregation errors also contribute.

\subsection{Regulation Effects}

The failure at the regulated Elizabethton site provides a clear negative result. Watauga Dam modifies the natural flow regime through storage, release, and peaking operations that create discontinuities in the discharge time series. Because these operations are governed by TVA management decisions---not by the meteorological or physiographic variables available to Hydra---the model cannot learn a consistent mapping from inputs to NWM residuals. When it attempts to correct, it introduces spurious adjustments that worsen the forecast. This finding aligns with \citeA{neisary2025}, who showed that post-processing regulated basins requires explicit reservoir storage and upstream dam operation features to achieve meaningful improvements.

\subsection{What Improves and What Does Not}

Across the unregulated sites, Hydra's largest gains occur during \textbf{high-flow events}, where NWM systematically underpredicts peak discharge. The regime-conditioned bias and multi-scale convolutions enable the model to add the missing volume during storms while leaving baseflow largely unchanged. This selective intervention is operationally valuable: for emergency management, more accurate peak-flow predictions can inform earlier and more targeted flood warnings.

In contrast, \textbf{low-flow and baseflow periods} show only modest improvements. This is expected: when NWM errors are small in absolute terms, the signal-to-noise ratio in the residuals drops, and the model correctly learns to apply near-zero corrections.

The \textbf{input ablation} clarifies the relative importance of each data stream. Lagged USGS observations are the single largest improvement driver: comparing the full USGS+NWM+ERA5 configuration to the gauge-free surrogate, adding gauge data improves RMSE reduction by 34 percentage points at Jefferson, 26 at Galax, and 51 at Sugar Grove (26--51 pp range). Adding NWM on top of USGS+ERA5 provides an additional 12--44 percentage point improvement, demonstrating that NWM forecasts contribute unique process information (particularly routing dynamics and spatial integration) that ERA5 alone cannot replicate. The two input streams are therefore complementary rather than redundant.

The \textbf{architecture ablation} shows modest improvements from the non-negativity constraint at Jefferson (RMSE reduction: 8.5\% $\to$ 11.4\%), while the base Hydra v3 performs best at Galax and Sugar Grove. This site-dependent pattern suggests the physics constraint is most beneficial where NWM errors are largest.

\subsection{Comparison to Prior Work}

The site-level gains reported here are numerically larger than those in several prior NWM post-processing studies, including \citeA{frame2021}, who reported median NSE improvement from 0.62 to 0.73 across 531 basins using LSTM post-processing. That comparison should be treated cautiously. The present study uses a small regional sample, a one-hour correction target, and lagged gauge observations that are not available in all operational settings. The more defensible contribution is therefore not a direct claim of outperforming prior work, but a diagnostic result: in these Appalachian basins, most one-hour correction skill comes from recent observed flow state, while NWM still adds complementary hydrologic information.

\citeA{han2022} achieved RMSE improvements of 15--25\% in the Russian River basin using an LSTM-based approach. Our gauge-free surrogate results (NWM+ERA5-only) are broadly comparable, and the stronger gains in the full USGS+NWM+ERA5 configuration underscore the importance of distinguishing telemetry-informed correction from gauge-free forecasting. That distinction matters because it changes both the operational interpretation and the appropriate benchmark.

\subsection{Comparison to Persistence Baselines}

A natural question is whether simpler methods could achieve comparable results. We evaluate two persistence baselines: (1) \textit{flow persistence}, $\hat{Q}(t) = Q_{\text{obs}}(t-1)$, and (2) \textit{residual persistence}, $\hat{Q}(t) = Q_{\text{NWM}}(t) + r(t-1)$.

Table~\ref{tab:persistence} compares Hydra and these baselines to NWM. Hydra reduces RMSE by 45--56\% relative to NWM, but both persistence methods achieve RMSE reductions of 71--92\% (NSE: 0.968--0.996), serving as upper bounds for 1-hour predictions given perfect gauge availability. This result reflects the strong autocorrelation of hourly streamflow: $Q(t-1)$ is typically an excellent predictor of $Q(t)$.

Hydra should therefore not be presented as the best point nowcast in this setting. Its value is instead as a physics-anchored residual-correction framework that remains tied to NWM and offers a foundation for settings where longer lead times, missing telemetry, or consistency with the baseline hydrologic model matter more than single-step point accuracy.

For operational one-hour nowcasting where only point predictions are needed, persistence establishes a stringent benchmark. The practical interpretation of Hydra is therefore complementary rather than replacement-oriented.

\subsection{Gauge-Informed Correction as Primary Contribution}

The central finding of this study is that gauge-informed correction achieves substantially larger improvements than input configurations without USGS data. The USGS+NWM+ERA5 configuration reduces RMSE by 45--56\% across the three unregulated sites, compared to 5--22\% for the gauge-free surrogate. This 26--51 percentage point gap demonstrates that lagged gauge observations are the dominant predictor of NWM errors.

The magnitude of this difference has practical implications. For near-real-time flood monitoring applications where USGS telemetry is available (typically within 15--60 minutes of observation), gauge-informed correction offers substantially better accuracy than configurations that rely solely on NWM and meteorological inputs. The input ablation experiments quantify this advantage: lagged USGS observations contribute 26--51 percentage points of RMSE reduction beyond the gauge-free surrogate, while NWM forecasts provide 12--44 points beyond what USGS+ERA5 alone achieves.

These complementary contributions suggest that both data streams encode distinct information. The lagged gauge observation captures the observed flow state---including the cumulative effects of antecedent conditions, routing delays, and unmeasured inputs. The NWM forecast encodes the model's physics-based projection of how that state will evolve, including spatial integration across the upstream network. Neither source alone captures both aspects, and their combination yields the largest improvements.


%-------------------------------------CONCLUSION-------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusions}

This study demonstrates that gauge-informed residual correction can substantially improve National Water Model streamflow accuracy at unregulated Appalachian catchments. The key findings are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Gauge-informed correction reduces error relative to raw NWM.} The primary USGS+NWM+ERA5 configuration reduces RMSE by 45.3\% at Jefferson (NSE: 0.43 $\to$ 0.83), 48.2\% at Galax (NSE: 0.27 $\to$ 0.80), and 56.0\% at Sugar Grove (NSE: 0.62 $\to$ 0.93) on the 2019--2020 test period.

    \item \textbf{Lagged gauge observations are the dominant predictor.} Input ablation shows that adding USGS data increases RMSE reduction by 26--51 percentage points beyond the gauge-free surrogate, demonstrating that observed flow state is the primary signal for next-hour NWM error correction.

    \item \textbf{Input sources are complementary.} The ablation experiments reveal that NWM forecasts contribute 12--44 percentage points of improvement beyond USGS+ERA5 alone, indicating that NWM provides unique process knowledge (routing dynamics and spatial integration) that complements gauge telemetry.

    \item \textbf{Largest gains occur during high-flow events.} Error decomposition confirms that Hydra's corrections are concentrated where NWM errors are largest (during storm events and peak flows) while preserving baseline accuracy during low-flow conditions.

    \item \textbf{A gauge-free surrogate still improves NWM.} The gauge-free surrogate configuration (NWM+ERA5-only) reduces RMSE by 5--22\% across all sites, providing a lower bound for scenarios where telemetry is unavailable.

    \item \textbf{Regulated sites require process-specific features.} The failure at the Watauga Dam site demonstrates that ML corrections without explicit dam operation data can be counterproductive, consistent with prior findings in the literature.

    \item \textbf{Persistence remains the stronger one-hour point benchmark.} In this setting, simple persistence baselines outperform Hydra for single-step point prediction, so the contribution of the proposed framework is a physically anchored correction layer rather than a replacement for autoregressive nowcasts.
\end{enumerate}

We acknowledge that with only three unregulated sites, these results should be interpreted as a regional proof-of-concept rather than as evidence of broad statistical generalizability. Future work should validate these findings across a broader geographic range.

Taken within that scope, the results support the conclusion that a lightweight, site-aware residual corrector can serve as a practical companion to the National Water Model for unregulated Appalachian catchments. The approach preserves the physical model's trajectory while systematically reducing bias during the high-flow events most relevant to flood forecasting. Future work should address regulated-site adaptation through explicit dam-operation features, extension to multiple forecast lead times, and testing across larger basin networks.
